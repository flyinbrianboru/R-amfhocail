Réamhfhocail · inscne · an ‘á’ · aidiachtaí iolra — notes, techniques and drills

1. Réamhfhocail — the system

The two worksheets look like lists to be memorised. They aren’t. There are two rules underneath, and between them they cut the memorising down to almost nothing.

Rule one: ar vs ag

Weather sits on you.
Possessions are at you.
  • ar — things that come over you and pass: ocras, tart, tuirse, codladh, eagla, fearg, náire, deifir, imní, ionadh, lúcháir, bród, éad, fonn — and illness: slaghdán, tinneas, bruitíneach, biseach.
  • ag — things you hold steadily: meas, cion, gean, grá, gráin, fuath, dúil, suim, spéis, muinín, iontaoibh, dóchas, aithne, eolas, cuimhne, gaol, trua.
The ten-year test. Could this be true of you for ten years? Then ag. You can respect someone for a decade; you cannot be hungry for one.

A few sit in both lists with different meanings: amhras ar X faoi Y (doubtful about a matter) versus amhras ag X ar Y (suspicious of a person).

Four of the six match English — faoi = about, le = with, i = in, ar = (directed) on. Only two don’t:

as — English says “proud of”, but think “out of”: the pride is made out of the thing. Same as as déanta as adhmad, as Béal Feirste, bain úsáid as.

roimh — English says “afraid of”, but roimh means “before” in the positional sense. Fáilte romhat and eagla roimhe feel like opposites, but they are the same posture: a stance toward something arriving. One greets it, one braces against it.

Adjectives inherit their noun’s preposition

Learn the noun pair and the adjective comes free:

  • bród as → bródúil as
  • imní faoi → buartha faoi
  • éad le → in éad le
  • cion ar → ceanúil ar
  • eolas ar → eolach ar

One verb, three prepositions

And the verbs that change meaning when the preposition appears: mhol sí mé = she praised me, but mhol sí dom = she advised me. D’éirigh mé = I got up, but d’éirigh liom = I succeeded. Lean mé é = I followed him, but lean mé den obair = I kept at the work.

Backwards from English

  • taitin le — the thing you like is the subject: taitníonn an ceol liom, the music pleases me.
  • teastaigh ó — the thing needed is the subject: teastaíonn airgead uaim.
  • teip ar — the failure lands on you: theip orm.
  • éirigh le — the success rises with you: d’éirigh liom.
  • punt ag … ar — the debt sits on the debtor: tá punt agam ort, you owe me a pound.
  • cuir ar — the clothes go on the wearer: chuir sí a hata uirthi.

Two traps worth naming. Anlann looks like it has the feminine -lann suffix but it is masculine — the -lann there is just an + lann, so an t-anlann. And an Béarla is masculine even though an Ghaeilge is feminine: different formation.

Semantic groups

  • Feminine: countries (an Fhrainc), rivers (an tSionainn), diseases (an bhruitíneach), languages in -is.
  • Masculine: days, months, metals, drinks, vegetables, timbers; most loanwords ending in a consonant.
  • The 2nd declension is almost entirely feminine, the 1st entirely masculine. Know the declension and you know the gender.

3. An ‘á’

á = do (or ag) + the possessive a, fused into one syllable. Irish has no present participle, so “being done” comes out as tá X á dhéanamh — literally “X is at its doing”. The thing acted on becomes a possessive in front of the verbal noun.

That is why the mutation carries information: the possessive still behaves like a (his / her / their), so the mutation tells you the gender and number of the thing being acted on.

á ól · á hól · á n-ól

masculine · feminine · plural — one syllable apart

  • The agent, if you want one, goes in ag: tá an aiste á scríobh agat.
  • The tense lives in the tá / bhí / beidh, never in the á.
  • Two or more things joined by agus count as plural: airgead agus am á gcaitheamh.
  • s has no eclipsis, so á scríobh looks the same in all three. Choose a different verb when you are testing yourself.

4. Aidiachtaí iolra

Two things are going on at once, and students usually only get one of them.

  1. Number agreement. After a plural noun the adjective takes its plural form: breá → breátha, maith → maithe.
  2. Lenition depends on the noun’s ending. Lenited when the plural noun ends in a slender consonant; not lenited when it ends in a vowel or a broad consonant.
The script hands you both cases a few lines apart: foclóirí breátha (vowel ending, no lenition) and focail chearta (slender l, lenition). If you can explain that pair you have the rule.

Where English has a proverb, Irish usually has its own rather than a calque. Use the native one — it is always the stronger answer.

  • Others front the key word with a: Cleachtadh a dhéanann máistreacht.
  • Alliteration does the memory work: múineann gá seift, bailíonn brobh beart, fan … fear mall.

7. Cúlra — why the vocabulary gap exists

  • 1607 — Imeacht na nIarlaí. The Gaelic aristocracy leaves, and so does the patronage that paid for the learned class. That class was the machinery for coining and standardising vocabulary.
  • 1609 onwards — Plandáil Uladh. The land base goes with it.
  • 19th century — Famine, emigration, the National Schools. Irish is left to the poorest and most remote districts.
  • The consequence: Irish stopped being built exactly when European languages were rapidly coining words for industry, science and government. The gap is a dated historical injury, not a defect in the language.

The tools: téarma.ie for technical terms (An Coiste Téarmaíochta, coining since 1927); foclóir.ie for ordinary modern usage; teanglann.ie for Ó Dónaill and Dinneen; logainm.ie for place-names.

Other languages did the same: Icelandic coined tölva for computer, from tala (number) + völva (prophetess) — a “number-prophetess”, and it stuck. Deliberate coinage works, but only when the word escapes the database and gets used. That is the whole argument of Tasc 5.

How to actually get this into your head

This material is pattern-based, not vocabulary-based, so it rewards short frequent drilling over long reading sessions. Ten minutes on the Cleachtaí tab beats an hour re-reading the notes.

1. Learn pairs as single words

Never learn bród and as separately. The unit is bród as, one word with a space in it, the way English speakers learn “proud of” without thinking about it. Say the pair aloud together every time: bród as, bród as, bród as. The same for eagla roimh, fiafraigh de, teip ar.

2. Use the ten-year test before you guess

Stuck on ar or ag? Ask whether it could be true of you for ten years. Respect, knowledge, kinship, trust — ag. Hunger, anger, hurry, a cold — ar. It is right nearly every time, and it is faster than recalling a list.

3. Remember that only two prepositions need memorising

faoi = about, le = with, i = in, ar = onto. Those map straight onto English. The whole learning burden is as (pride, shame, trust — think “out of”) and roimh (fear, welcome — think “facing what’s coming”). Two items, not forty.

4. Chant the pronoun rows

The prepositional pronouns are a rhythm, not a table. Say each row aloud as one burst, seven beats, without looking:

orm · ort · air · uirthi · orainn · oraibh · orthu

Do one preposition a day in the car or walking. Within a fortnight the whole grid is automatic, and the Tasc 3 gapped sentences stop being puzzles. When a row is solid, test it out of order using the Cleachtaí tab.

5. Substitution drills

Take one sentence and run it through all seven persons without stopping:

Tá eagla orm roimhe. Tá eagla ort roimhe. Tá eagla air roimhe…

Then swap the second half: …roimhe, roimpi, romhainn, rompu. This is how the forms become reflexes rather than lookups. It is also the single most useful thing you can do out loud while doing something else.

6. Make the mutation audible

The á rule is invisible on the page until you say it. Drill the three-way contrast aloud:

á ól · á hól · á n-ól

Masculine, feminine, plural. Do the same with á thógáil / á tógáil / á dtógáil. If you can hear the difference you will write it correctly; if you only see it, you will guess.

7. Translate into Irish, not out of it

Reading the Irish and understanding it feels like progress and is mostly an illusion — recognition is far easier than production. Cover the Irish column and go English → Irish. The Aistriúchán drill does this and makes you mark yourself honestly. Be strict: a missing h is wrong.

8. Use the proverbs as anchors

A proverb is a whole grammatical structure you can carry around in one piece. Ní fhanann trá le fear mall gives you fan le and a negative present in one breath. Is de réir a chéile a thógtar na caisleáin gives you the autonomous and a fronted clause. Learn eight proverbs properly and you have eight structures permanently available.

9. Work your weak list, not the whole deck

The drill tracks what you get wrong and builds a Laga deck from it. Reviewing things you already know is comfortable and nearly worthless. Spend your time where the errors are.

10. Space it out

Short sessions beat long ones, and gaps beat cramming. A reasonable pattern for a weekly sheet:

  • Day of class — read the notes once, drill the new topic for ten minutes.
  • Next day — drill the same topic again. It will feel worse than yesterday. That is the point.
  • Three days later — mixed drill including older topics.
  • Weekly — a Tráth na gCeist across everything, and read the weak-item list.

11. Write the mutation clue first

In a gapped exercise, read the lenition before you read the gap. __ bhean can only be den bhean. __ chrann can only be den chrann. __ Shíle can only be do Shíle. The sheet leaves the mutation in place and it gives the answer away — but only if you look for it.

12. Keep the dialect columns straight

You are learning Ulster Irish from material that includes Munster and Connacht forms. When you meet a new form, file it in one of three columns — Ulaidh, Caighdeán, elsewhere — rather than as a free-floating word. Writing doiligh in an essay is fine; writing conas alongside it is not, because the mixture is what marks a learner.
